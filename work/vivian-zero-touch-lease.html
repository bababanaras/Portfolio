The problem

Leasing asks renters to type it all in, then asks staff to do it again

Most leasing software is a set of forms in front of a set of queues. The renter fills in an application, staff re-key it into the next system, and every exception waits for someone to notice it. We ran discovery with operators, walking the application-to-lease process from both the staff side and the renter side.

What staff told us

  • Leases get assembled by hand. Parking concessions and addenda are attached one at a time, then signed documents are uploaded into the property management system manually.
  • Every application looks the same in the queue until someone opens it, so the risky file and the clean one wait in the same line.
  • Screening, signing and payment live in separate tools, and the handoffs between them are where files stall.

What renters run into

  • The same details, typed again and again across inquiry, application and lease.
  • Fees that read alike. An application fee and a holding fee mean different things and are easy to confuse.
  • Households that don't fit one form. Co-applicants, children and guarantors each need something different from the flow.

The bet

The conversation is the interface

Instead of a smarter form, Vivian runs the whole lease and the screens appear when the conversation needs them. Leasing and residency are one journey, so the same conversation keeps going after move-in.

One conversation, many channels

Web chat, text and voice are views of the same conversation with Vivian, not three products.

Collect as you go, confirm once

Vivian gathers what the application needs while answering questions, so the last step is a review and one tap.

Rails, not a blank box

Reply buttons keep the flow moving. Typing in your own words always works too.

Leave and come back

The conversation outlives the browser tab. Nothing is asked twice.

Identity at the moment of commitment

Browsing needs nothing. Contact details and verification appear when a price is held or money moves.

A person when it matters

Vivian handles the file first. Staff get it, with the full conversation, when judgement is needed or the renter asks.

How it evolved

Six iterations, from a checkout form to a lease inside the chat

Each step is a working prototype, not a static mock. Pick one to see what we built, what it taught us and what changed next.

Form-first checkout

What we built
Apply, status, and sign and pay as a three-step checkout. Vivian sat in a side rail, checking income against the threshold and offering to autofill the form.
What it taught us
The renter still did the data entry. Vivian was helping with a task the product shouldn't have asked for in the first place.
What changed next
Move the front half of the job into the conversation.

Chat qualifies, form closes

What we built
Vivian ran inquiry, matching, tour or skip, pre-qualification and identity and income checks in chat, then handed over a prefilled application. On a phone that became a four-card page. Text and email links opened the same chat on a phone.
What it taught us
The handoff was the seam. Everything after it turned back into a form, and the renter switched from talking to filling in.
What changed next
Keep the renter in the conversation all the way to signature.

The whole lease in one conversation

What we built
Lease review, signing and payment moved into the chat, with a file panel beside it showing everything Vivian had collected. Then a first-touch version for a stranger arriving from the marketing site, with no name, no phone and no unit.
What it taught us
A stranger needs answers before a price, and a price before an ask. Five household shapes were more than a first-touch flow could carry.
What changed next
Vivian asks before she prices, collects contact details only when she first needs them, and the flow carries three household shapes instead of five.

One session, every view

What we built
Usability testing showed the widget and the full view were two different conversations: expanding replayed a stripped copy. We rebuilt it as one session that every view frames: the widget, expanded, a pop-out window, the standalone chat and the phone.
What it taught us
Continuity is the product. Any seam between views reads to the renter as starting over.
What changed next
Twelve numbered fixes, from expand-in-place by default to a resume link and a one-tap re-price when a quote lapses.

Approval isn't binary

What we built
Approved with a condition became a designed path: a co-signer or a third-party rent guarantee compared side by side, the home held during review, and a deposit alternative at move-in.
What it taught us
The riskiest moment for a zero-touch lease isn't the decline, it's the maybe. Without a path through it, every conditional approval turns into a phone call.
What changed next
Eight guarantee and deposit cases, each reachable in the prototype, including the guarantee declined and the renter who leaves mid-review.

The operator's side

What we built
Applications Vivian completes land in one review queue with the file beside the list. Escalations arrive with the conversation attached.
What it taught us
Staff trust a score only when they can see what's behind it, and a decline has legal weight, so it has to carry a reason.
What changed next
Per-property auto-approval thresholds and policy rules decide what Vivian completes on her own.

Inside the current design

The decisions that make zero-touch hold up

One conversation, every view

The widget is not a smaller copy of the chat. It is the chat, mounted once and reframed as the renter expands it, pops it out or opens it on a phone.

  • Expanding in place is the default. Nothing is copied or replayed.
  • The offer to expand appears once, on the first rich card, and says what you gain.
  • Declining is answered once and remembered for the session.
  • A blocked pop-up quietly falls back to expanding in place.
  • Closing a pop-out window hands everything back to the widget, with a marker in the thread.
  • On a phone, the full view is its own page, and leaving returns to the listings.

Ask for identity only when it's needed

No account, no password, no sign-up wall in front of the listings. The first time Vivian needs to reach the renter, to hold a price or send a quote, she says why and asks inside the thread. A one-time code confirms the contact. Coming back later rides the resume link, so the renter is never asked to log in to continue.

The lease arrives in the conversation

The lease is a message and a document at once: the PDF, a tab for each addendum, and signing progress. Every addendum traces back to something the renter said, such as a parking stall, a dog or the move-in special, and Vivian says so. Initials sit on the pages that create obligations, and each leaseholder signs on their own link.

Approval isn't a yes or a no

Screening often comes back approved with a condition. Vivian states the condition in plain words and puts both ways through side by side: a co-signer the renter knows, or a third-party rent guarantee, each with fee, cover and timing. The home and the quote are held while the guarantee is reviewed. At move-in, a deposit alternative sits next to the deposit with what is due today. A decline comes with a plain reason, the formal notice, and a person.

A person, without starting over

Asking for a human is always one tap away. Vivian names who and when, and passes the conversation so the renter repeats nothing. On the operator side the escalation lands with the whole thread attached, not as a cold task.

Coming back days later

Renters rarely finish in one sitting. The conversation is kept longer than the price lock, so a renter who returns after five days picks up where they left off. One who returns after the quote lapsed gets a one-tap re-price instead of starting again.

Every edge case has a switch

If a case can't be reached in a demo, it doesn't get built. The prototype puts each one behind an edge-case menu, so product, engineering and QA test the same states we designed.

From availability page to move-in

    The operator's side

    Staff work the exceptions, not the queue

    Applications Vivian completes land in one review queue, labelled with where they came from. The file opens beside the list, so a leasing manager never loses their place. Two rules hold everywhere: a score never appears without its breakdown, and a decline needs a written reason, because it sends a formal notice to the applicant. Auto-approval thresholds and policy rules are set per property, which decides what Vivian completes on her own and what comes to a person.

    What's next

    Linear first, then configurable

    Leasing rules change from province to province and state to state. Version 1 ships one linear path, the same steps for every renter, so it stays predictable while we learn where operators need to bend it.

    Next comes a flow each operator shapes with their own policies, with rent-table items such as parking and pets priced inside the conversation instead of added by staff afterwards.

    What I'd tell the next team

    The widget isn't a preview of the product. It is the product.

    Our first widget ran its own small conversation and replayed a copy when expanded. A usability pass caught it, and the rebuild made one session that every view frames.

    Ask at the moment of need, and say why.

    Browsing stays open to anyone. The request for contact details lands when the renter has a reason to give them.

    If QA can't reach a case, it doesn't exist.

    Putting every edge case behind a switch turned the prototype into the spec engineering builds from.
