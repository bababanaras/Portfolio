Oaken Digital: banking that customers over 60 can finish on their own.

I led the redesign of Oaken's digital banking platform, turning a costly, assisted model into a digital-first, zero-touch experience for a customer base where nearly 80% are over 60.

Context

Why this mattered to the business

Oaken's assisted banking model was expensive to run and hard to scale. This work made it digital-first and zero-touch for the people who actually bank with Oaken.

By removing friction for older customers, we cut operational dependency and grew trust in self-service. It also proved a point to the business: clear, data-driven UX drives revenue efficiency, lowers risk and supports long-term growth.

What is Oaken Financial?

Oaken is a Canadian direct-to-consumer savings and investment brand, owned by Home Trust and now part of Fairstone Bank. It offers high-interest savings accounts with no monthly fees or minimum balance, and Guaranteed Investment Certificates (GICs) in registered (TFSA, RSP, RIF) and non-registered forms.

Problem

Built for confident users. Used by customers over 60.

The platform was optimized for digitally confident users, but nearly 80% of active customers were over 60. The result was confusion, high abandonment and very few straight-through account openings.

Only 20% of account openings were zero-touch.

If we left it unsolved

  • Account opening would keep leaning on call centres and branches, pushing up operating costs.
  • Older customers would lose trust in digital channels and fall back to assisted banking, or leave.
  • Oaken would never scale as a true digital-first bank, capping growth and profitability.
  • Manual interventions and incomplete applications would raise regulatory and compliance risk.
Research

What digital-first banks were getting right

What they did well

  • Mobile-first, task-focused journeys with few decision points
  • Plain language, progressive disclosure and strong visual hierarchy
  • Speed, reassurance and transparency during onboarding
  • Success measured by completion rates, not feature depth

How they designed it

  • Linear, predictable flows with clear progress indicators
  • Prominent, unambiguous calls to action ("Continue", "Finish application")
  • Fewer choices per screen to reduce cognitive load
  • Contextual help, inline validation and real-time feedback

Competitor benchmarking

We benchmarked leading digital banks on account-opening steps and time to complete, language complexity and readability, error handling and recovery, and how clearly the dashboard surfaced the primary task.

Competitors optimized for confidence, not intelligence. That was our gap.

Discovery

Understanding why customers were failing, not just where

Gaps in the existing journey

  • Dense information architecture with unclear priorities
  • Several competing calls to action, causing decision paralysis
  • Non-linear flows that broke users' confidence
  • An assumption that users already knew how digital banking works

Deep behavioural analysis

Funnel tracking told us where people dropped off. We needed to know why. So we went a level deeper:

  • Funnel drop-offs at every onboarding and origination step, which isolated identity verification, document upload and long-form data entry as the hardest moments.
  • Time on task, to find steps that demanded too much effort or caused hesitation.
  • Form-level abandonment, field error rates and backtracking, to pinpoint unclear instructions and validation issues.

A single view of user behaviour

To make this repeatable, we spent a year building Power BI dashboards that combined API-level events, platform telemetry and third-party analytics, desktop and mobile interaction data, and drop-off trends by device, step and cohort. They gave the whole team one trusted view, faster diagnosis and data-backed priorities.

The core issue was clarity and flow, not a lack of intent.

Strategy

Four principles to design for zero assistance

Clarity over cleverness
Plain language and obvious next steps beat novel patterns.
One primary action per screen
Every screen asks for one decision, and only one.
Progressive disclosure
Complexity appears only when the user needs it.
Design for zero assistance
If someone would need to call us, the screen has failed.

A new, predictable journey

  • Linear and predictable from start to finish
  • Explicit about what happens next
  • Reinforced with reassurance and progress cues
Insights

Jobs to be done

"When I open a bank account online, I want to feel confident I'm doing it right, so I don't need to call anyone."
"When I log in, I want to immediately see what matters most, without figuring things out."

These shaped three pieces of work

  • A simpler onboarding experience
  • A decluttered dashboard with clear primary actions
  • A redesigned application and origination flow

How we redesigned onboarding, the dashboard and origination

  • Step-by-step onboarding with visible progress
  • Large type, high contrast and plain language
  • Wireframes reviewed early with business, compliance and engineering to avoid late friction

Final UI

Calm, clear and built for confidence

The final UI turned research and jobs-to-be-done into confident, readable interfaces for older users, without falling behind modern digital banking standards.

Typography and readability
Larger base sizes, generous line height and less jargon. A clear hierarchy from heading to supporting to helper text.
Visual hierarchy and focus
One dominant call to action per screen, and whitespace used to guide attention.
Spacing and layout
More room between interactive elements and a steady vertical rhythm, so every screen feels predictable.
Feedback and error prevention
Inline validation, reassurance at critical steps like identity checks, and clear success states.
Consistency across channels
A shared design system and responsive layouts that keep hierarchy intact on small screens.
Design system
Accessibility-first patterns: scalable type, contrast-safe colour tokens, larger targets and reusable onboarding and origination components.

Together these made the experience feel simple, calm and trustworthy, which is what zero-touch completion depends on.

Validation

Testing, iterating and shipping what we designed

Testing and iteration

  • Usability testing with older users to check comprehension and confidence
  • A/B tests on call-to-action language, layout density and step order
  • Ongoing analytics to track drop-off and completion speed
  • Refinements based on real behaviour, not opinions

Handshake with engineering

To keep design intent from eroding in build, we worked with engineering early and throughout:

  • Frontend and backend engineers joined discovery to check feasibility and surface constraints early.
  • Detailed specs, annotated flows and component documentation removed ambiguity.
  • The design system became a shared language, with tokens, components and states mapped directly to code.
  • Regular design reviews during sprints protected visual and interaction fidelity and accessibility.
  • Edge cases, error states and responsive behaviour were worked through together for desktop and mobile parity.

What we designed is what shipped.

Leadership

My role and how I led

As Senior UX Manager I owned the end-to-end experience across onboarding, authenticated banking and originations, from strategy through execution to outcomes, bridging user needs with business and compliance constraints.

Team leadership

  • Led and mentored a cross-functional UX team around a shared vision of clarity and accessibility.
  • Set design principles and success metrics so the team could make confident decisions on its own.
  • Moved critique from opinion to evidence with data-informed sessions.

Stakeholder management

  • Partnered with Product, Engineering, Compliance, Legal, Analytics and Operations from day one.
  • Used journey maps, JTBD and funnel data to align everyone on real pain points.
  • Raised risk and compliance concerns early to prevent late rework.

Decisions and trade-offs

  • Chose clarity over feature richness, deliberately.
  • Used behavioural data and experiments to settle disagreements.
  • Balanced short-term goals with long-term scale and inclusivity.

What I learned

  • Designing for the most vulnerable user makes the experience better for everyone.
  • Data and empathy need each other: analytics tells you where, users tell you why.
  • Great banking UX is less about innovation and more about removing doubt.
